There is a particular kind of frustration that comes from
knowing something is true but not yet having the right words for it. I
have lived with that frustration for most of my adult life.
I am not a conventional institutional physicist. I attended the
University of California, Berkeley from 1968 to 1973, completing
dual majors in Chemistry and Cytology, and left refusing the degrees
on principle. What I am is a Natural Philosopher — someone who
looks at the world and asks the oldest question there is: What is this,
really? What is matter? What is space? What is the strange fact that
you and I are here, aware of our own existence, looking out at a
universe that seems indifferent to us and yet somehow produced us?
Those questions have led me on a decades-long investigation
that has taken me from the notebooks of Nikola Tesla to the
blackboards of David Bohm, from the biology labs of Rupert
Sheldrake to the particle accelerators of modern quantum physics.
Slowly, through years of reading and thinking and arguing with
myself at three in the morning, a picture began to emerge.
The universe is not a machine. It is a memory.
I do not mean that metaphorically. I mean it as precisely and
literally as I can state it. The fabric of space itself stores information
– the patterns of everything that has ever happened, every form that
has ever organized itself, every creature that has ever lived. And that
information does not simply sit there inertly, like text in an unused
library. It actively shapes what happens next. The universe learns
from its own history. It has habits, not just laws. And those habits
grow stronger every time they are repeated.

A NOTE ON MATHEMATICS
In the chapters that follow, you will encounter equations. If you
are someone for whom mathematical symbols are a source of
anxiety, I want to reassure you: these equations are here to serve you,
not to intimidate you.
Mathematics is the language of precision. When we write an
equation, we are not hiding the truth; we are stating it as clearly and
unambiguously as human language allows. An equation is a sentence
that cannot be misunderstood.
In this book, every equation is placed in a dedicated box,
accompanied by a detailed, word-by-word translation into plain
English. We explain what every symbol means, why the relationships
are structured the way they are, and what the equation predicts about
the real world.
You can skip every equation box in this book and still
understand the narrative flow, the conceptual framework, and the
experimental evidence. The mathematics is the scaffolding; the ideas
are the building. Enjoy the architecture, whether or not you choose
to inspect the joints.

PART ONE: THE LAST THREE SECONDS
The mud is cold.
That is the first thing — always the first thing — the cold, black

of things. Through the stitching of his coat. Through the gap where
his collar meets his neck. Through the marrow of his shin bones, his
femurs, his spine.
He is running.
His breath comes in great, ragged plumes — white clouds torn
apart by his speed, by the wind that cuts across the open ground like
something alive and furious. The air smells of smoke and wet stone
and something underneath those, something darker and older,
something he has no name for but that lives in the belly and turns it
liquid with fear.
To his left: a low stone wall, ancient, mortared with generations
of silence. To his right: open ground, and beyond that, the shape of
buildings wrapped in fog. Somewhere behind him — forty yards,

THE STANDARD ACCOUNT of quantum mechanics,

must be simple, but that a physical theory fundamentally based on
probability and that forbids the question “what is really happening?”
is not a complete physical theory. It is a calculation recipe, not an
explanation.

what is actually happening. The papers were called “A Suggested
Interpretation of the Quantum Theory in Terms of Hidden

CHAPTER THREE: Space vs. Counterspace
You and I were taught that space is the stage. Matter and energy
are the actors. Space is the empty container through which things
move – it existed before anything was in it, it will exist after
everything has left it, and it has no qualities of its own beyond pure
extension and geometry. Space is what remains when everything is
removed.
This assumption is so deeply embedded in our scientific culture
that it barely registers as an assumption at all. It feels like a
description of obvious fact.
But there is a profoundly different way of thinking about space
– not as an absence but as a presence, not as a container but as a
participant, not as the stage but as an actor. This alternative was
developed in the early twentieth century by a small tradition of
geometers working at the intersection of mathematics, philosophy,
and physics, and it has extraordinary consequences for the morphic
field theory we are building in this book.
That alternative is called counterspace.
—
TO UNDERSTAND COUNTERSPACE, we first need to
appreciate something about ordinary space that is usually omitted
from the curriculum: Euclidean space has a dual.
In mathematics, duality is a precise concept. Two mathematical
structures are dual to each other when you can take one and, by
systematically exchanging certain elements, obtain the other. The
XXXV

The Memory of the Universe

result is not an approximation or a metaphor. It is an exact
mathematical transformation that preserves all the structural
relationships of the original.
The duality of Euclidean geometry – the geometry you learned
in school – appears in projective geometry. And in projective
geometry, there is a perfect symmetry between points and planes.
This symmetry is called the principle of duality.
In ordinary geometry, we think of space as built from points. A
line is a collection of points. A plane is a collection of lines, which
are collections of points. Three-dimensional space is built upward
from points – the smallest possible element, the irreducible atom of
the geometric world.
The principle of duality says: you can flip this entire
construction over. Instead of building space upward from points,
build it inward from planes. In this dual picture, a point is not a tiny
location in space. A point is the intersection of all planes that pass
through it.
Read that slowly: a point is the intersection of all planes that
pass through it.
In ordinary space, a point is the minimum. It is the smallest
thing, with no extent and no internal structure. It is the emptiest
element of geometry.
In the dual picture – in counterspace – a point is a maximum. It
is the gathering of all planes – infinitely many planes, one for every
possible direction – that converge at that location. The point is rich,
not empty. It is the most informationally dense element of the
geometry, not the least.

This inversion changes everything about how we think about

IN ORDINARY EUCLIDEAN SPACE, fields radiate outward
from their sources.
Think of a bonfire: heat and light radiate in all directions from
the center. Think of a light source: photons fly outward from the lamp
in every direction, filling space with radiant energy that weakens
with the inverse square of distance. Think of a gravitational field: in
Newtonian mechanics, gravity is a force that radiates from a massive
body, pulling nearby objects toward it while diminishing in strength
with the square of the distance.
These are all centrifugal fields: fields organized around a center
from which their influence expands outward. The source is at the
center; the effect spreads outward into the surrounding space.
In counterspace, the geometry runs in exactly the opposite
direction. Fields in counterspace do not radiate outward from a point.
They converge inward toward a point from a surrounding boundary.
Think of a drain in a bathtub: water does not radiate outward
from the drain. It converges from all directions toward the drain. The
drain is not a source but a sink – a point of convergence, not emission.

comes out of the center. Everything goes in. The surrounding water
organizes itself around the central point by converging from the
boundary of the whirlpool, drawn inward by the pressure differential
between the periphery and the center.
